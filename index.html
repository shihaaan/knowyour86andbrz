Know Your 86 & BRZ

Loving cars and understanding cars are two different skills. This is the second one, built around one car with four badges: the Toyota 86, Toyota GT86, Scion FR-S and Subaru BRZ. Every idea here is general and applies to any petrol car — but every example is yours, so the knowledge has somewhere to stick.

01

One car, four badges

If you drive a BRZ, everything in this guide is about your car too. Here's exactly where the two diverge.

In 2008 Toyota bought a stake in Subaru's parent company, and the two then co-developed this car: Toyota led the design and the injection system, Subaru engineered the engine and built the cars. The result was sold under four names, and people call them the "Toyobaru twins".

Toyota ZN6

  • 86 — Japan, Australia, New Zealand and most markets
  • GT86 — Europe and the UK
  • Scion FR-S — North America, 2013–2016, then renamed Toyota 86
  • Named after the 1983–87 Corolla AE86

Subaru ZC6

  • BRZ — all markets
  • Built by Subaru in Gunma, same line as the Toyotas
  • Same FA20 engine, gearbox, differential and brakes
  • Generally arrived with more standard equipment

What is genuinely the same

The engine, the gearbox, the differential, the brakes, the body structure and the suspension design. Reviews at launch described them as sharing engine, chassis and brakes, with the main differences being appearance inside and out. So every section of this guide — the engine, the fluids, the alignment angles, the maintenance habits — applies to a BRZ exactly as it does to an 86.

What actually differs

  • Suspension tune. This is the real one. Subaru fitted stiffer front springs and softer rear settings, and tuned its dampers differently. Australian reporting at launch put the first-generation difference at roughly 10% stiffer front coil springs in the BRZ, and described the on-road difference as subtle rather than significant. The intended effect was a BRZ that leans a fraction less and feels slightly more stable, with a mild bias toward understeer, while the Toyota is a touch more willing to rotate.
  • Standard equipment. It varied by market, but the BRZ typically launched with more kit: 17-inch alloys where a base 86 had 16s, dual-zone climate control against the Toyota's manual air conditioning, a multi-function steering wheel, push-button start, and an information display.
  • Front bumper, lights, badges and wheel designs. Cosmetic.
  • Who you talk to. Warranty, recalls and parts go through Subaru rather than Toyota — which matters a lot for the recall in section 13, because the two brands ran separate campaigns with different build-date windows.
How to read the rest of this guide

Where something applies to every version, it's written plainly. Where a car-specific detail matters, there's an orange On your car box. Where the Toyota and the Subaru genuinely differ, the box says so. One warning on researching your own car: be careful not to mix up the generations. The 2022-onward GR86 and second-generation BRZ use a bigger 2.4-litre FA24 engine and different figures. Specifications you find online for those cars do not apply to a first-generation car.

02

The big picture

Petrol goes in one end, rotation comes out the other. Everything else is management.

Almost every mechanical part of your car exists to do one of three jobs: make rotation, manage rotation, or stop rotation. The engine makes it. The clutch, gearbox, driveshaft and differential manage it. The brakes and tyres stop it. Hold onto that and the rest of this guide is just detail.

In plain English

An engine is a machine that turns small explosions into a spinning shaft. It cannot do anything else. It can't start from rest, it can't reverse, it can't idle and move at the same time. Every other part in the chain exists to cover for one of those limitations.

03

The engine

Four pistons, lying on their sides, doing the same four jobs forever.

Inside one cylinder

Before the cycle, the hardware. This is a cut-through of a single cylinder, which is the basic unit your engine has four of.

The four-stroke cycle

A "stroke" is one full travel of that piston. Your engine takes four strokes to complete one cycle of work, which is why each cylinder only produces power a quarter of the time.

Why this matters

Because only one stroke in four makes power, a single-cylinder engine would deliver its push in lumps. Four cylinders firing at staggered intervals means something is always pushing, which is why multi-cylinder engines are smooth.

Why "boxer" is the whole point of this car

Most four-cylinder engines stand their cylinders upright in a row — an "inline-four". Yours lies them flat, two pointing left and two pointing right, pistons punching away from each other like two boxers trading jabs. Hence boxer, or flat-four, or "horizontally opposed".

This flat shape is not a styling choice. A flat engine is a short engine, so all that mass sits much lower in the car. Low mass means a low centre of gravity, and that is the single biggest reason this car changes direction the way it does.

Bore, stroke and where the name comes from

Two numbers describe the shape of a cylinder:

  • Bore — the diameter of the cylinder. 86 mm.
  • Stroke — how far the piston travels from top to bottom. 86 mm.

When bore equals stroke, engineers call it a square engine. A wide, short-stroke engine ("oversquare") loves revs because the piston doesn't have to travel far. A narrow, long-stroke engine ("undersquare") makes torque low down but runs out of breath. Square sits deliberately in the middle.

Multiply the bore's area by the stroke, times four cylinders, and you get 1,998 cc — the engine's displacement, the total volume the pistons sweep through.

On your car

The 86 mm × 86 mm figure is not an accident. The Toyota is a tribute to the 1983–87 Corolla AE86, the rear-drive coupé that became a drift legend, and the bore and stroke were both designed to land on 86 mm — with an 86 mm exhaust tip to match. The BRZ shares the engine, so a Subaru's cylinders are also named after a Toyota. BRZ, for its part, is usually explained as Boxer engine, Rear-wheel drive, Zenith.

Compression ratio, and why you must use 98

Compare the cylinder's volume when the piston is at the bottom to its volume when the piston is at the top. That ratio is the compression ratio: 12.5:1, which is high for a naturally aspirated road engine.

Squeezing the mixture harder before you light it extracts more energy from the same fuel — more power and better economy at once. The catch is knock (also called detonation or pinking): under high pressure and heat, petrol can ignite on its own before the spark plug fires, which fights the rising piston instead of pushing it down. Sustained knock destroys engines.

A fuel's octane rating (RON in New Zealand, Australia and Europe) measures its resistance to igniting under pressure. Higher octane is not "more powerful" fuel — it's more stubborn fuel. A 12.5:1 engine needs stubborn fuel.

Don't do this

This engine is specified for 98 RON. The knock sensors will protect it on 95 by retarding the ignition timing, so it won't blow up, but you lose power and efficiency — often enough that the cheaper fuel costs you more per kilometre. Regular 91 is a bad idea in this engine.

Valves, camshafts and the timing chain

Each cylinder has four valves — two to let the mixture in, two to let the exhaust out. They're pushed open by egg-shaped lobes on spinning rods called camshafts. There are two camshafts per cylinder bank, four in total, which is what DOHC means: double overhead camshaft.

The camshafts must stay perfectly synchronised with the pistons, or a valve will be open when a piston arrives and the two will meet. A timing chain keeps them in step. Some engines use a rubber timing belt that must be replaced on a strict interval or it snaps and wrecks the engine. Yours uses a chain, designed to last the life of the engine. That's one expensive job you don't have to budget for.

The engine also has variable valve timing (Subaru's AVCS). The ideal moment to open a valve at 1,500 rpm is wrong at 7,000 rpm, so the engine rotates the camshafts slightly on the fly to suit the revs. It's the reason a modern engine can idle smoothly and breathe at high rpm.

D-4S: two sets of injectors

Fuel has to be sprayed into the engine, and there are two schools of thought about where:

  • Port injection sprays into the intake tract before the valve. The fuel has time to mix with air evenly, and it washes over the back of the intake valves on the way past.
  • Direct injection sprays at very high pressure straight into the cylinder. More precise, more power, better economy — but the intake valves never get washed, so oily vapour from the engine's breathing system bakes onto them as carbon over tens of thousands of kilometres.

Toyota's D-4S system fits both and lets the computer choose, or blend them, depending on load and revs. You get direct injection's power with port injection's self-cleaning. This is genuinely clever engineering, and it's why this engine doesn't suffer the carbon build-up that plagues many direct-injection-only engines of the same era. It was also the unusual part of the collaboration: Toyota shared an in-development injection system with Subaru, who redesigned their boxer around it.

Naturally aspirated, power, and torque

Your engine is naturally aspirated: air arrives at whatever pressure the atmosphere provides. No turbocharger or supercharger forcing extra air in. The upside is response — the throttle does exactly what your foot does, instantly and proportionally. The downside is that the only way to make more power is to rev harder.

Which brings us to the two numbers everyone quotes and almost nobody separates properly:

  • Torque is twisting force. It's how hard the engine can shove. Measured in newton-metres (Nm).
  • Power is torque multiplied by revs. It's how fast the engine can do work. Measured in kilowatts (kW) or horsepower.

Torque is what you feel in your back. Power is what determines how quickly you actually get down the road. An engine making modest torque at very high revs can make serious power — exactly what this one does.

On your car — the famous torque dip

Between roughly 3,500 and 4,500 rpm the FA20 makes noticeably less torque than it does either side. Every 86, GT86, FR-S and BRZ of this generation does this; it's a known consequence of the intake and exhaust tuning, not a fault.

What it means for you: if you short-shift at 4,000 rpm, the car feels flat and slow, and you'll wonder what everyone sees in it. Hold a gear past 4,500 and it comes alive and keeps pulling to 7,400. Learning to use the top half of the rev range is the single biggest change to how this car feels, and it costs nothing.

04

Clutch and gearbox

The engine only works in a narrow band of revs. Gears hide that from you.

Why gears exist at all

Your engine is useful between roughly 1,500 and 7,400 rpm. Below that it stalls; above that it destroys itself. That's a speed range of about 5:1. But your wheels need to go from 0 km/h to over 200 km/h, and they need enormous force to get 1,240 kg moving from rest and very little to hold it at 100 km/h.

A gearbox resolves that mismatch. It's a torque multiplier, and it works by the oldest trade in mechanics: you can have force or you can have speed, and you buy one with the other.

Reading a gear ratio

A ratio like 3.63:1 means the engine turns 3.63 times for every one turn of the gearbox output. You get 3.63 times the torque at the output and a third of the speed. That's first gear: huge shove, not much road speed. Sixth is close to 1:1 or lower — almost no multiplication, so the engine can sit lazily at low revs while you cruise.

There's a second multiplication after the gearbox, in the differential, called the final drive ratio. It applies in every gear. Your total gearing in any gear is the gearbox ratio multiplied by the final drive — so a gearbox ratio of 3.63 with a final drive of about 4:1 gives roughly 14 times engine torque at the wheels in first. Both numbers are listed in the specifications pages at the back of your owner's manual; worth finding once.

The clutch

The engine must keep spinning when the car is stationary, so you need a way to disconnect them. In a manual car that's the clutch: a friction disc between the engine's flywheel and a spring-loaded pressure plate.

Pulling away means deliberately letting the disc slip — engine spinning faster than the gearbox — and feeding the two together. Slipping converts the speed difference into heat and wears the friction material, which is why:

  • Riding the clutch (resting your foot on the pedal while driving) keeps it half-engaged and cooks it.
  • Holding the car on a hill with the clutch instead of the brake burns through a clutch faster than anything else you can do.
  • A clutch that slips — revs rise but the car doesn't accelerate to match — is worn out, not adjustable.
On your car — the cold second gear

These cars use an Aisin six-speed manual. It's a strong gearbox, but when properly cold it commonly resists going into second — the lever feels like it hits a wall. The synchroniser rings have to drag two spinning shafts to the same speed, and thick cold gear oil makes that slow.

Don't force it. Wait a couple of minutes until the oil warms, shift to second more slowly and deliberately, or go first-to-third for the first kilometre. Forcing a cold synchro is how you eventually pay for a gearbox.

05

Drivetrain and differential

Front engine, rear-wheel drive — and one very clever gearbox in the back.

Your car is FR: front engine, rear-wheel drive. The engine sits at the front, the gearbox directly behind it, and a spinning driveshaft (or propshaft) runs down the tunnel between the seats to a differential between the rear wheels, which sends drive out sideways to each one.

Why a differential has to exist

Drive into a corner and your outside rear wheel travels along a bigger arc than the inside one. Over a tight turn that difference is significant. If both wheels were locked to one solid shaft, one of them would have to skip and scrub across the road, which would make the car hop, wear the tyres and eventually break something.

A differential is a small gearbox that lets the two wheels turn at different speeds while still driving both. It "differentiates" between them. That's the whole name.

Open diffs, and the problem with them

A basic open differential has a flaw that follows directly from how it works: it always sends equal torque to both sides, which means the maximum it can send to either side is limited by whichever wheel has less grip. Lift one rear wheel off the ground and it spins uselessly while the other does nothing. Put one wheel on a wet painted line and you get the same outcome in miniature.

The limited-slip differential

A limited-slip differential (LSD) allows the speed difference a corner needs, but limits it — once one wheel starts spinning much faster than the other, internal friction fights that difference and torque is biased toward the wheel that still has grip.

These cars use a Torsen LSD, short for "torque sensing". It uses worm gears whose geometry naturally resists being driven backwards, so the harder one wheel tries to run away, the more the unit locks up. Mechanical, immediate, no electronics. Fitment varied by market and trim, so if you're not certain whether yours has one, check the specification for your exact model and build.

On your car

Two practical consequences. First, both rear wheels pull you out of a corner, so you can use the throttle mid-corner instead of just waiting. Second, when the back does step out, it does so with both wheels together and progressively, which is a large part of why these cars have a reputation as a brilliant place to learn car control — on a track or a skid pan, not on the road.

An LSD also needs its own specific oil. See the fluids table: the wrong spec in a limited-slip diff causes chatter and wear.

06

Suspension and chassis

The job is not comfort. The job is keeping four small rubber patches pressed against the road.

People assume suspension exists to smooth out bumps. Comfort is a side effect. The real job is keeping the tyres in contact with the road surface while the car's body does whatever its mass makes it do. A tyre that's momentarily light, or leaning over on its edge, isn't gripping — and everything your car does depends on grip.

The three components that do the work

  • Spring. Carries the weight of the car and compresses over bumps. Its stiffness is its spring rate, in newtons per millimetre. A stiffer spring resists roll and dive but transmits more of the road.
  • Damper (shock absorber). Controls how fast the spring is allowed to move. A spring on its own, once disturbed, would bounce for ages — the damper forces oil through small valves, turning that motion into heat and killing the oscillation. Worn dampers are why an old car feels floaty and skips over mid-corner bumps.
  • Anti-roll bar. A torsion bar linking the left and right wheels of an axle. When both wheels move together it does almost nothing, but when one side rises relative to the other — body roll — it twists and resists. It lets a designer cut roll without making the ride harsh.

Holding all of it to the car are rubber bushes. They allow controlled movement and absorb vibration, and they perish with age and heat. On a car from 2012, tired bushes are one of the most common reasons one of these feels vague compared to how it felt new.

Centre of gravity and weight transfer

These cars' centre of gravity sits around 460 mm off the ground — remarkably low for a road car, and the direct payoff of that flat engine mounted low and rearward. Kerb weight is about 1,240 kg, with roughly 53% of it over the front wheels.

Low and light changes the car's behaviour through one mechanism: weight transfer. Whenever you accelerate, brake or turn, load shifts across the car, and the amount it shifts depends on how high the centre of gravity is. A tall car has to lean, settle, and only then turn. Yours transfers less, so it changes direction almost immediately and stays flat while doing it. That's the feeling people mean when they call these cars "pointy" or "honest".

The single most useful thing to understand about driving

Braking moves weight onto the front tyres; accelerating moves it onto the rears. More load means more grip, up to a point. So the front tyres have their most grip while you're braking and their least while you're accelerating — and the reverse for the rears.

That's why smooth inputs matter: every abrupt movement of the controls is a sudden redistribution of grip. And it's why braking gently into a corner's entry makes the nose turn in better. Every advanced driving technique is an application of this one idea.

Understeer and oversteer

  • Understeer — the front tyres run out of grip first. You turn the wheel further and the car keeps going straight on. The instinctive reaction (more steering) makes it worse; the fix is to ease off and let the front tyres start gripping again.
  • Oversteer — the rear tyres run out of grip first. The back slides wide and the car rotates more than you asked for.

Nearly all road cars are deliberately set up to understeer at the limit, because running wide is more survivable for an untrained driver than spinning. These cars are unusual: modest power, a well-balanced chassis and deliberately low-grip standard tyres mean the rear will let go willingly and predictably.

Toyota vs Subaru, at the limit

This is the one place the twins genuinely diverge. Subaru's stiffer front springs and softer rear tune were intended to make the BRZ a fraction more stable and slightly more understeer-biased, where the Toyota is marginally more willing to rotate. Reviewers who drove them back to back at launch generally described the difference as subtle rather than transformative, and it shows up at the limit on a track rather than on a normal road. Neither is better; they're two readings of the same brief.

Worth saying plainly

That willingness to rotate is a gift on a track or a skid pan with run-off and an instructor. On a public road it's a crash. If you want to learn what your car does at its limits — and with this car, you should — find a track day or a defensive driving course. New Zealand has plenty of both.

07

Steering and alignment

Three angles decide how your car tracks, turns and wears its tyres.

Rack and pinion

Turning the wheel rotates a shaft with a small gear on the end — the pinion. Its teeth mesh with a long toothed bar, the rack, which slides left or right and pushes tie rods that swivel the front wheels. Rotation in, sideways movement out. That's the whole mechanism.

Your car has electric power steering (EPS): an electric motor assists the rack. It's lighter and uses no engine power when you're going straight, which is why nearly every modern car uses it. It's also the one component of this generation that reviewers consistently criticised — the steering is accurate and quick, but the EPS filters out some of the texture a hydraulic system would pass through. Knowing that it's a characteristic of the system, not a fault in your car, saves you chasing it.

The three alignment angles

  • Toe — whether the wheels point slightly inward or outward, looking down from above. Toe-in adds straight-line stability; toe-out sharpens the initial response into a corner. Get it wrong and you scrub rubber off your tyres with every kilometre.
  • Camber — how much the wheel leans in or out, looking from the front. Negative camber (top leaning inward) looks odd standing still, but as the body rolls in a corner the loaded outside tyre is pushed upright onto its full tread instead of rolling onto its outer shoulder.
  • Caster — the backward tilt of the steering axis, viewed from the side. It's what makes the steering wheel want to return to centre on its own, and what makes the car track straight without constant correction. Same principle as the castor wheel on a shopping trolley.

08

Brakes

Brakes don't remove energy. They convert it into heat and then get rid of the heat.

A moving car holds kinetic energy, and energy can't be deleted. Braking converts it into heat, by rubbing friction material against spinning steel, and then sheds that heat into the air. Everything that goes wrong with brakes is a heat problem.

The hydraulic chain

Your foot can't generate enough force to stop a car, so the system multiplies it:

  1. The pedal pushes a rod into the master cylinder, via a vacuum servo that multiplies your effort — this is why the pedal goes hard when the engine isn't running.
  2. That pushes brake fluid through steel lines. Liquids don't compress, so the push arrives at the other end essentially undiminished.
  3. At each wheel the fluid pushes pistons in a caliper, which squeezes two pads against the spinning disc.
  4. Friction between pad and disc turns motion into heat, which the disc sheds into the air.
Why brake fluid has a replacement interval but no wear

Brake fluid is hygroscopic: it absorbs water out of the air, slowly, through seals and hoses, even if you never drive. Water in the fluid lowers its boiling point.

That matters because hot brakes heat the fluid closest to them. If it boils, you now have compressible vapour in a system that only works because liquid doesn't compress — so the pedal goes long and soft and the brakes stop working, usually at the worst possible moment. Fluid is replaced on time, not on kilometres, because what degrades it is the calendar.

Fade, and the two kinds of it

  • Pad fade. The pad material gets hotter than its design range and its coefficient of friction falls. The pedal still feels firm but the car won't slow as hard. Common on long descents and track sessions.
  • Fluid fade. The fluid itself boils, as above. The pedal travels further and feels spongy. More serious.

Pad choice is a real trade-off, not a quality ladder. Street pads bite well from cold and are quiet. Track pads need to be hot before they work properly and chew through discs when cold — fitting them to a daily-driven car makes it worse at the thing it does 95% of the time.

The electronics: ABS, TRC and VSC

  • ABS (anti-lock braking). A locked wheel is a skidding wheel, and a skidding wheel can't steer. ABS senses a wheel about to lock and releases and re-applies that brake many times a second. In an emergency stop: press hard, keep pressing, and steer. The grinding noise and pedal pulsing are it working.
  • TRC (traction control). Stops the driven wheels spinning under power by cutting throttle or braking the spinning wheel.
  • VSC (vehicle stability control). Compares where you're steering with where the car is actually going, and if they disagree, brakes individual wheels to rotate the car back into line.

These cars have a VSC Sport mode that raises the intervention thresholds, and allow the systems to be switched off entirely. The off switch is part of what makes them good cars to learn in — in a controlled environment, with space, and ideally supervision.

09

Wheels and tyres

Every force your car can produce passes through four patches of rubber the size of your palm.

This is the most under-appreciated fact in car ownership. Engine, brakes, suspension, steering, stability control — all of it is funnelled through four contact patches. Tyres are the highest-leverage thing on your car and the easiest to neglect.

Reading the sidewall

The date code nobody checks

Somewhere on the sidewall is a DOT code ending in four digits. The first two are the week, the last two are the year: 3621 means the tyre was built in week 36 of 2021. Rubber hardens and loses grip as it ages regardless of tread depth. A tyre with plenty of tread and seven years on it is a worse tyre than its tread suggests — and on a car from 2012 that may have sat around, it's worth a look.

Pressure

Check pressures cold — before driving, or at least a couple of hours after. Driving heats the air inside and raises the reading, so topping up warm tyres leaves you underinflated once they cool.

  • Underinflated: the sidewall flexes too much, the tyre squirms under steering, both edges of the tread wear faster than the middle, and the flexing builds heat — which is how tyres fail at speed.
  • Overinflated: harsh ride, a smaller contact patch, and wear concentrated down the centre strip.

A cheap gauge and five minutes a month improves how your car drives more than most money you could spend on it.

On your car — the tyres are a deliberate choice

These cars were fitted with narrow, modest-grip touring tyres on purpose. With 147 kW and low-grip rubber, the car reaches its limits at speeds a human can process, and tells you clearly as it gets there. That's the entire design brief: a car that teaches.

Fitting much grippier tyres is the most effective single upgrade for lap times and wet-weather safety, and it genuinely transforms the car — but understand the trade. Higher limits arrive faster, with less warning, and the car becomes less communicative at road speeds. Plenty of owners deliberately keep modest tyres for exactly that reason. Either choice is defensible; drifting into one without realising is not.

Fluids

Six liquids keep your car alive, and for each one the specification matters more than the brand.

"Oil is oil" is the most expensive wrong belief in car ownership. Every fluid below has a specification your car was engineered around. Meeting that specification is what matters; which reputable brand you buy mostly doesn't.

Toyota or Subaru

The specifications below are the same fluids for the same hardware, but the two makers sell them under their own names and part numbers — Toyota Genuine against Subaru Genuine. Either brand's product, or any oil meeting the same standard, is what the part needs. Use whichever your workshop stocks; just match the standard, not the badge.

The one habit that matters most on this engine

The FA20 is known for using some oil between services — more than you'd expect from a modern Toyota or Subaru. It isn't necessarily a fault, but it means you cannot assume the level is fine just because the car was serviced recently.

Low oil plus sustained hard cornering is the known way these engines die. In a corner the oil in the sump slides to one side; if the level is already low, the pump can momentarily draw air instead of oil, and the bearings run dry. Check the dipstick at least monthly, and always before a spirited drive or a track day. Warm the engine, park level, shut it off, wait about five minutes for the oil to drain back, then check. This single habit is the cheapest engine insurance you will ever buy.

11

Electrics, briefly

Four parts explain almost every electrical problem you'll meet.

  • Battery. A 12-volt store of energy. Its real job is huge current for a few seconds to spin the starter, plus running your electronics when the engine is off. Batteries fail from sitting unused as much as from use.
  • Starter motor. A powerful electric motor that spins the engine fast enough for it to start running on its own. One click and nothing usually means it isn't getting enough current — often a flat battery or a corroded terminal rather than a dead starter.
  • Alternator. Driven by a belt off the engine. Once running, it powers everything and recharges the battery. If the battery light comes on while driving, you're running on stored charge only, and you have limited time.
  • Fuses and relays. A fuse is a deliberate weak point that melts before your wiring does. If one thing stops working, check its fuse before assuming the worst. There's a fuse box under the bonnet and another in the cabin; your owner's manual maps both.
On your car

These cars have a small battery, part of the weight-saving brief, and one that sits for a fortnight can struggle to start. If yours isn't a daily driver, a cheap trickle charger or battery maintainer is worth more than a new battery every two years.

12

Maintenance you can actually do

Five minutes of looking beats a thousand dollars of fixing.

Every fuel stop · 60 seconds

  • Walk around the car. Look at all four tyres — one soft tyre is obvious if you look.
  • Glance under the car where it was parked for fresh drips.
  • Notice any new noise, smell or vibration from the last drive. New symptoms are cheap; ignored symptoms aren't.

Monthly · 10 minutes

  • Engine oil level on the dipstick (see fluids for how).
  • Tyre pressures, cold, against the door-jamb placard. Include the spare if you have one.
  • Coolant level in the translucent reservoir — cold engine only.
  • Brake fluid level in its reservoir. A falling level means a leak or worn pads.
  • All lights, including brake lights and indicators. Get someone to watch, or use a reflection.
  • Tread depth — the legal minimum in New Zealand is 1.5 mm, but wet grip drops off well before that.

On schedule · book it in

  • Engine oil and filter. Your manufacturer's interval is in the back of your owner's manual, commonly 10,000–12,000 km or 12 months, whichever comes first. If you drive it hard or track it, halve it.
  • Brake fluid about every two years.
  • Air filter and cabin filter per the schedule, sooner on dusty roads.
  • Spark plugs — iridium, long-life, around 100,000 km.
  • Coolant — long first interval, shorter after.
  • Gearbox and diff oil aren't on the normal schedule, but many owners change them around 100,000 km, and much sooner if the car is tracked.
  • Wheel alignment after any kerb strike, new tyres, or if it pulls or wears unevenly.
  • Tyre rotation. On a rear-drive car the front and rear tyres wear differently; swapping front-to-back on the same side evens it out, if your tyres are the same size and not directional.
Two documents worth an hour of your time

Your owner's manual contains the fluid specs, capacities, intervals, fuse maps and the dipstick procedure for your exact car. If it's missing from the glovebox, the PDF is easy to find — search for the owner's manual for your model, year and brand.

Your service history tells you what's actually been done, and on a car this age that's worth more than the odometer reading. If you don't have it, start one today: a cheap notebook with date, kilometres and what was done.

13

Known quirks of a first-generation car

What's a fault, what's a characteristic, and the one thing you should check this week.

Check this one first — the valve spring recall

Both makers recalled early first-generation cars over valve springs that could fracture. A valve spring's job is to close the valve; if one breaks, the valve can stay open, meet a piston, and destroy the engine. The fix is free, and it is the single most important thing to verify about any early car of this family.

The windows are different for each brand, so check against yours:

  • Toyota. Toyota New Zealand recalled 349 Toyota 86s built between 10 April 2012 and 16 May 2013. Toyota Australia ran the equivalent campaign over cars produced April 2012 to July 2013.
  • Subaru. Subaru's campaign covered 2013 model year BRZs built between 9 December 2011 and 2 July 2013 — an earlier start than the Toyota window — alongside various Impreza, XV and Forester models. In the US it ran as recall WTY-84; in Australia as campaign SI0491.

What to do: find your VIN (base of the windscreen on the driver's side, on the door jamb sticker, and on your registration papers) and give it to a dealer for your brand — Toyota for an 86, GT86 or FR-S, Subaru for a BRZ. Not all cars in a production range were affected, so the VIN is the only way to know. If your car came into the country as a used import, this matters even more: the work may have been done overseas, or not at all. Get the answer in writing for your service file.

Oil consumption

Covered in fluids, and the most important ongoing habit on this engine. These engines use oil; check the level monthly and before hard driving.

Cold second gear

A stiff, baulky shift into second when the gearbox is cold is normal for this car. Warm it up, shift slowly, or skip to third for the first kilometre. Not a fault, and not something to force.

The fuel pump chirp

Many of these cars make a faint rhythmic chirping or cricket-like noise from the rear when stationary. It's a known characteristic of the fuel pump assembly on this generation and generally harmless. Note that the fuel pump recall you'll find online applied to 2018–19 cars, not early first-generation ones — different issue, different years.

The torque dip

Not a fault. See the engine section. Rev it out.

Everything that's simply 13 years old

Independent of any model-specific issue, a car of this age with original parts will have tired suspension bushes, dampers past their best, brake fluid that may never have been changed, an aging battery, perished rubber hoses, and possibly tyres that are old even if they look fine. None of these are dramatic failures. Collectively they're most of the difference between one of these that feels sharp and one that feels tired — and they're also where your money goes furthest.

Glossary

Every term in this guide, in one place. Type to filter.

ABS
Anti-lock braking system. Releases and re-applies a brake rapidly to stop a wheel locking, so you can still steer.
Alternator
Belt-driven generator that powers the car's electrics and recharges the battery while the engine runs.
Anti-roll bar
Torsion bar linking left and right wheels on an axle. Resists body roll without stiffening the straight-line ride.
Aspect ratio
The second number in a tyre size. Sidewall height as a percentage of tread width.
AVCS
Subaru's name for its variable valve timing system, fitted to the FA20.
Bore
The diameter of a cylinder. 86 mm on this engine.
Boxer / flat-four
Engine layout with cylinders horizontal, opposing each other. Short and low, which lowers the car's centre of gravity.
BRZ
Subaru's version of this car, chassis code ZC6. Usually explained as Boxer, Rear-wheel drive, Zenith.
Caliper
The clamp at each wheel that houses the pistons and squeezes the brake pads onto the disc.
Camber
How far a wheel leans in or out when viewed from the front.
Camshaft
Rotating shaft with lobes that push the valves open at the right moment.
Caster
Backward tilt of the steering axis viewed from the side. Gives self-centring and straight-line stability.
Clutch
Friction disc that connects and disconnects the engine from the gearbox. Clamped at rest; the pedal releases it.
Compression ratio
Cylinder volume at the bottom of the stroke divided by volume at the top. 12.5:1 on this engine.
Contact patch
The area of tyre actually touching the road. Roughly palm-sized, times four.
D-4S
Toyota's injection system using both direct and port injectors, giving direct injection's power without its carbon build-up.
Damper
Shock absorber. Controls how quickly the spring is allowed to move, turning that motion into heat.
Differential
Gearset that lets the two driven wheels turn at different speeds through a corner while still driving both.
Displacement
Total volume swept by all the pistons. 1,998 cc on this engine.
DOHC
Double overhead camshaft. Two camshafts per cylinder bank, one for inlet valves and one for exhaust.
DOT code
Moulded tyre marking ending in four digits: week and year of manufacture.
EPS
Electric power steering. An electric motor assists the steering rack instead of a hydraulic pump.
FA20 / 4U-GSE
This car's engine. Subaru's code and Toyota's code for the same 2.0-litre flat-four.
FA24
The 2.4-litre engine in the 2022-onward GR86 and second-generation BRZ. Different car, different figures.
Fade
Loss of braking from heat — either the pad material losing friction, or the fluid boiling and going compressible.
Final drive
The fixed gear ratio inside the differential, applied in every gear on top of the gearbox ratio.
FR layout
Front engine, rear-wheel drive. The front wheels only steer; the rear wheels only drive.
FR-S
The North American version, sold under Toyota's Scion brand from 2013 to 2016, then renamed Toyota 86.
GT86
The European and UK name for the Toyota version.
Hygroscopic
Absorbs water from the air. Why brake fluid is replaced on time rather than mileage.
Knock
Fuel igniting from pressure and heat before the spark fires. Destructive; resisted by high-octane fuel.
LSD
Limited-slip differential. Allows the speed difference a corner needs but limits it, biasing torque to the wheel with grip.
MacPherson strut
Suspension design where the damper forms part of the wheel upright. Used at the front of these cars.
Naturally aspirated
No turbo or supercharger. Air enters at atmospheric pressure. Linear response; power comes from revs.
Octane / RON
A fuel's resistance to igniting under pressure. These cars are specified for 98 RON.
Oversteer
Rear tyres lose grip first. The back slides wide and the car rotates more than you asked.
Power
Torque multiplied by engine speed. How quickly work gets done. 147 kW at 7,000 rpm.
Spring rate
How much force it takes to compress a spring a given distance. Newtons per millimetre. The main tuning difference between the Toyota and the Subaru.
Stroke
How far the piston travels between top and bottom. 86 mm, equal to the bore.
Timing chain
Links the crankshaft to the camshafts so valves and pistons never collide. Yours is a chain, not a belt — no replacement interval.
Toe
Whether the wheels point slightly in or out viewed from above. Affects stability, turn-in and tyre wear.
Torque
Twisting force. How hard the engine can shove. 205 Nm at 6,400–6,600 rpm.
Torsen
Torque-sensing limited-slip differential using worm gears. Fitment varied by market and trim.
Understeer
Front tyres lose grip first. You add steering and the car runs wide anyway.
Variable valve timing
Shifting when the valves open and close to suit engine speed.
VIN
Vehicle identification number. Your car's unique serial, used to check recalls and order correct parts.
Viscosity
How thick an oil is. In 0W-20, the 0W describes cold flow and the 20 describes thickness when hot.
VSC
Vehicle stability control. Brakes individual wheels and cuts throttle when the car isn't going where you're steering.
Weight transfer
Load shifting between tyres as you brake, accelerate or turn. The basis of nearly every driving technique.
ZC6
The chassis code for the first-generation Subaru BRZ.
ZN6
The chassis code for the first-generation Toyota 86, GT86 and Scion FR-S.

No terms match that.
